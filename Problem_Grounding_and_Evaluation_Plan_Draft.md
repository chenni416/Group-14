# Evaluation Plan Draft

Selected Collaboration Problem: Unequal contribution / "free-riding" in group projects — division of labor is unclear from the start, and there is no shared, visible record of who did what, so conflict or resentment builds up but surfaces too late to fix.

Tool concept (for context): An AI-assisted task allocation and contribution-tracking tool. The AI suggests how to break a project into sub-tasks and flags when contribution logs look uneven; a human team member (e.g. the team lead) always makes the final call on task assignment and on any conflict raised.

Success definition — We will know our tool works if teams using it can, at any point mid-project, produce a clear, agreed-upon answer to "who is responsible for what, and who has actually done their part" in under one minute — without a status meeting. Because our tool keeps a human in the loop (the team lead approves task assignments and reviews contribution flags rather than the AI deciding autonomously), success has to be measured as: the AI's task-breakdown and contribution-flag suggestions are useful enough, often enough, to be worth the interruption of showing them to the user — not that the AI's suggestions are always correct on their own.

Target users — Groups of 3–5 university students currently working on a real, ongoing group assignment (e.g. a course project or club deliverable). We will access them through our own class sections and student club networks, recruiting teams who are willing to use the tool for at least one full project cycle (2–3 weeks) rather than a single session, since free-riding problems typically only become visible over time.

Method — A combination of structured observation and a brief interview: we will observe 2–3 team check-in sessions where the tool's task list and contribution log are actually used, then follow up with a short (10–15 minute) interview with each team near the end of the project cycle, asking specifically whether the tool changed how/when they noticed unequal contribution and what they did about it.

Minimum evidence threshold — At least 3 out of 5 pilot teams report, in the follow-up interview, that the tool helped them catch or address an unequal-contribution issue earlier than they would have without it (i.e. before it became a source of open conflict), AND at least 2 of those teams show observable evidence in their contribution logs (not just self-report) that task reassignment or a direct conversation happened as a result of a tool-generated flag.

# Problem Grounding

Who specifically has the collaboration problem you are addressing?
A Taiwanese university sophomore or junior assigned to a 3–5 person group project for a course — someone who is not the natural "team leader" type, tends to go along with whatever the group decides in the first LINE message, and ends up either doing far more than their share (because they're too conflict-avoidant to call it out) or quietly doing less than their share (because no one ever explicitly assigned them a task and they assume someone else will cover it).

What do they currently do instead of your tool?
They rely on an unstructured LINE group chat to figure out who's doing what — tasks get mentioned in passing ("誰要弄PPT?") rather than formally assigned, no one writes down who agreed to what, and progress is only checked informally, usually the night before the deadline. When contribution feels unequal, the default response is to say nothing and either silently absorb the extra work or silently under-deliver, rather than raise it directly — because bringing it up mid-project feels confrontational and socially costly.

What would be observably different about their collaboration if your tool worked?
Instead of task ownership living only in scattered chat messages, there would be a single visible list showing exactly who is responsible for what, updated as the project progresses — so at any check-in, a team member could point to it and say "this is what's mine, this is what's yours" without it feeling like a personal accusation. When contribution becomes visibly uneven, the tool's flag — not a teammate's direct confrontation — would be what surfaces the issue, which lowers the social cost of raising it. Observably: team check-ins would reference the shared task/contribution log instead of relying on memory or LINE scrollback, and reassignment or clarifying conversations would happen during the project (traceable to a tool-generated flag) rather than only surfacing as resentment after the deadline has passed.
